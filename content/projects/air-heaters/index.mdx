Designed and manufactured stainless steel enclosures for four high-temperature air heater modules at TESLAB. The system simulates jet engine environments, achieving 750°C and 85 SCFM at 60 psi.

## Project Background

This project was completed for the [Turbomachinery & Energy Systems Lab, under Dr. Daejong Kim](https://www.uta.edu/academics/faculty/profile?user=daejongkim#Research). The rig is used to test air foil bearings in high temperature and pressure conditions seen in jet engines. I engineered a custom high-temperature heater system to meet temperature, pressure, and airflow requirements. Commercial solutions were cost-prohibitive and most lacked the required flowrate.

## Design

I received preliminary designs for multiple heater core models that would be combined to achieve different maximum temperatures and flow rates. With the cores ordered, I was responsible for designing manufacturable enclosures.

### Initial Approach & Pivot

I began using **parametric modeling in SolidWorks**, initially planning to cut sheet metal to size and manually weld boxes together. After consulting with our department machinists and running cost estimates in Excel, I realized that **sheet metal bending would be significantly more cost-effective** than my original approach.

This led me to learn SolidWorks' **sheet metal features**, iterating between 3D enclosure designs and 2D flat patterns to identify and resolve manufacturing issues before fabrication.

### Insulation & Thermal Considerations

When sizing the enclosures, I accounted for the **thickness of fiberglass insulation**, which was selected for its low cost and ease of use. Since the insulation's thermal performance relies on trapped air, I ensured the design would prevent compression that would reduce its effectiveness.

### Final Design & DFM

After reviewing [SendCutSend's design-for-manufacturing (DFM) guidelines](https://sendcutsend.com/services/cnc-bending/#resources), I simplified each enclosure into **two-half clamshell boxes**, allowing easy insertion of heater cores with insulation.

**Key DFM considerations for sheet metal:**
- **Bend radii:** Adhered to minimum radii based on material thickness to prevent cracking
- **Tolerances:** Designed to not need the limits of the manufacturing process.
- **Material thickness:** Selected 0.06" SS 304 for balance of stiffness and cost.
- **Corner relief:** Added relief cuts to prevent material tearing at corners

I selected **SS 304 stainless steel** for its weldability and high-temperature oxidation resistance. Corners were designed with **TIG welding in mind** for structural reinforcement.

## Manufacturing & Assembly

SendCutSend delivered precision parts with no issues, and assembly went smoothly **except for one critical error on my end**: I accidentally mirrored the bends on one enclosure. Rather than scrapping the part, I solved this by **inverting the heater core assembly**, leveraging the modular union-nut connections I had designed for serviceability.

### Heater Core Assembly

I collaborated with university machinists to refine the heater core design for welding. The assembly uses **stainless steel tubes with drilled holes** acting as manifolds to distribute airflow across multiple heating elements, then recombine at the output.

- **Thermocouple integration:** Added a port for K-type thermocouples secured with flared fittings from McMaster-Carr.
- **Serviceability:** Implemented **union joints** on 2 of the 4 heaters, allowing quick replacement of heating elements without disassembling the entire module.

## Controls

I designed the control system around **PID temperature controllers** with K-type thermocouple inputs, driving **solid-state relays (SSRs)** for precise heating control.

**Safety & electrical design:**
- **Manual override switch** for instant heater shutdown.
- **Individual fuses** in each control box for overcurrent protection.
- **Distributed electrical loads:** With a total draw of up to 8.4 kW, I distributed heaters across multiple **20A circuits**, each with individual breakers.
- **SSR cooling:** Added a small fan to the main controls enclosure to prevent overheating.

## Integration & Testing

After heater cores were welded, I conducted **empirical insulation testing** by measuring heater output temperature against the exterior surface temperature of the insulation.

**Results:**
- **500°C heaters:** 1 layer of fiberglass insulation sufficient
- **750°C heaters:** 2 layers required for safe exterior temperatures

I performed comprehensive commissioning tests:
1. **Individual heater testing:** Each unit ran at maximum temperature for 5+ minutes
2. **Full system test:** All heaters operated simultaneously to verify electrical capacity and thermal performance
3. **Thermal monitoring:** Used a thermal camera throughout all tests to monitor heaters, control boxes, and surrounding surfaces for hotspots.

## Other Contributions

### Reverse Engineering
I reverse engineered the wiring and controls of a existing set of heaters that used 3-phase power. Converted the circuit to use use the same PID controllers and SSRs. This involved tracing the wiring, identifying components, and adding new mounting and connections.

### Air Flow Monitoring
I also created the system to split and monitor the air flow to each of the heaters according the the PI's requirements. This involved sourcing and installing the correct adapters for the existing flow meters, and learning how to set them up for the target flow rates. Everything was routed to minimize the tripping hazard.
